\begin{abstract}
Models that predict antimicrobial resistance from bacterial genomes are usually evaluated on
random splits, where related strains fall on both sides. We re-evaluated genomic resistance
prediction in 6,962 \textit{Escherichia coli} genomes and seven antibiotics in fourteen
pre-registered experiments. On a random split, strain metadata alone reached an average
precision of 0.705, against 0.850 for the best genomic model. When whole sequence types were
held out, a pan-genome catalogue failed for cephalosporins and gentamicin, and adding each
strain's AMRFinderPlus calls raised normalised average precision from 0.450 to 0.726. Assembly
size predicted resistance within sequence types, tracked accessory gene content rather than
submitting study, and carried no signal in 10,232 external isolates. Models trained on our
strains transferred to that cohort. A sparse logistic regression on the determinant calls matched
gradient boosting internally and beat a fixed determinant rule externally (+0.104), where gradient
boosting transferred worse; harmonising labels to EUCAST breakpoints widened that margin. In
\textit{Campylobacter}, whose determinants are nearly monogenic, no model beat the rule, and very
major error rates exceeded regulatory benchmarks for every approach. Genomic predictors should be
reported against a determinant rule, an assembly-only model and a lineage-disjoint external test.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

Whole-genome sequencing is increasingly used to infer antimicrobial susceptibility, either by
looking up known resistance determinants or by training a model on genotype--phenotype pairs.
Machine-learning studies routinely report high accuracy, but bacterial collections are highly
structured: closely related strains share both their genomes and, often, their resistance
phenotypes. When such strains are split at random between training and test sets, a model can
score well by recognising lineages rather than resistance mechanisms. Yu, Wheeler and Barquist
showed across five pathogens that biased sampling driven by population structure confounds
machine-learning prediction of resistance \cite{yu2025biased}, and benchmarks that compare learned
models with the rule-based ResFinder report that the ranking of the two depends on how related the
test genomes are to the training genomes \cite{hu2024assessing,bortolaia2020resfinder}.
Genotype--phenotype discordance is itself drug- and species-specific \cite{cheon2026discordance},
and even a well-curated determinant catalogue leaves part of the resistance in \Ecoli{}
unexplained, most notably for amoxicillin--clavulanic acid \cite{lipworth2024uncatalogued}.

Three questions follow that a single random-split benchmark cannot answer. First, how much of a
model's apparent accuracy comes from information that is not resistance biology at all: strain
metadata, study of origin, or properties of the assembly? Second, when lineages are held out,
does prediction fail because the model cannot generalise, or because the features it is given do
not contain the relevant determinants? Third, once the determinants are present, does a learned
model add anything over the obvious alternative, a fixed rule that calls a strain resistant when a
known determinant is detected?

We address these questions on the public collection of 6,962 \Ecoli{} genomes with
phenotypes released by Arcadia Science \cite{arcadia2024dataset,bell2025arcadia}, an
independent cohort of 10,232 isolates from NCBI Pathogen Detection \cite{ncbi_pathogen_detection},
their minimum inhibitory concentrations, and a second species. Every analysis that bears on a
claim was pre-registered: the endpoint and its pass rule were committed to a public repository,
with a timestamp, before the relevant data were read, and every outcome is reported, including
those that failed. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item a decomposition of random-split accuracy into metadata, assembly and genomic signal, and a
        test of whether the assembly signal is a batch artefact or biology;
  \item a pre-registered test that isolate-specific determinant calls rescue prediction on unseen
        lineages, with its coverage limits stated;
  \item a frozen external validation on a cohort from different countries, sources and years,
        with a leave-one-BioProject-out audit and harmonised MIC-based labels;
  \item a comparison of learned models against a determinant rule in two species, showing that a
        sparse linear model on determinant calls is sufficient where a model helps at all, and
        identifying where it does not.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Materials and methods}

\subsection*{Training collection and labels}
The training collection is the Arcadia Science \Ecoli{} release \cite{arcadia2024dataset}:
6,962 genomes with susceptibility phenotypes for seven antibiotics used throughout, ampicillin
(AMP), ciprofloxacin (CIP), amoxicillin--clavulanic acid (AMC), trimethoprim--sulfamethoxazole
(SXT), gentamicin (GEN), cefotaxime (CTX) and ceftazidime (CAZ). Intermediate calls were counted as
resistant in all primary analyses and excluded in a sensitivity analysis. The collection is
concentrated in the United Kingdom and Norway (91\% of strains) and spans 2001--2017
(Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}). Multilocus sequence types (ST) and phylogroups were taken from the
release. \todo{Confirm how ST was assigned in the release and cite it.}

\subsection*{Features}
\textbf{Pan-genome catalogue.} Gene presence/absence over the release's reference pan-genome
catalogue, rebuilt from the release's PLINK files after we found that the distributed matrix
encoded the minority allele rather than presence; the rebuild changed only the orientation of
affected loci, and every result here uses the rebuilt matrix. \todo{Give the number of loci
flipped and point to the repair report in the supplement.}

\textbf{Determinant calls.} For each strain, the AMRFinderPlus \cite{feldgarden2019amrfinder,
feldgarden2021amrfinderplus} calls published by NCBI Pathogen Detection (software 4.2.7, database
2026-03-24.1), matched by sequencing run and BioSample. Calls without a suffix were taken as
acquired genes, \texttt{=POINT} calls as point mutations, and partial, end-of-contig,
mistranslated and HMM-only calls were not counted. Genes and mutations present in at least five
strains were kept (89 acquired-gene and 82 point-mutation columns). Calls were available for
5,072 of 6,962 strains (72.9\%); the strains without calls are not treated as lacking
determinants but are excluded from every analysis that uses calls.

\textbf{Metadata and assembly statistics.} Country, collection year, submitting study, sequencing
platform, and five assembly statistics: assembly length, coding-sequence count, GC content, contig
count and contig N50.

\subsection*{Models}
\textbf{Classical and neural stacks.} The classical arm stacks five base learners on gene and SNP
features with a logistic-regression meta-learner; the full stack adds a multi-branch neural network
(three expert encoders with a gating network) as a sixth base learner. Its architecture is given in
Supplementary Methods \todo{write}; it is evaluated here only as one of the compared models.

\textbf{Gradient boosting on determinant calls.} XGBoost \cite{chen2016xgboost} with fixed settings
(400 trees, depth 6, learning rate 0.05, subsample 0.8, column subsample 0.5), no tuning, one model
per drug. ``Acquired-only'' uses the acquired-gene columns; ``all calls'' adds the point mutations.

\textbf{Sparse linear model.} L1-penalised logistic regression (scikit-learn
\cite{pedregosa2011sklearn}, liblinear, $C=1$), on the same binary features, with no tuning.

\textbf{Determinant rule.} A strain is called resistant if it carries a determinant from a fixed
list per drug (Table~\ref{tab:rule}), written from known mechanisms before any rule score was
computed. The rule needs no training.

\textbf{Assembly arm.} Histogram gradient boosting on assembly length, contig count and N50 only.

\subsection*{Evaluation splits}
Random split; a held-out sequence-type split in which 103 STs (1,045 strains) never appear in
training (``lineage holdout''); ST-grouped five-fold cross-validation in which each ST is whole in
one fold; random five-fold cross-validation; two country splits (United Kingdom $\rightarrow$
Norway and the reverse); and a temporal split within Norway (training before 2013, test from 2013).

\subsection*{Metrics and uncertainty}
Because test prevalence differs between splits and drugs, we report prevalence-normalised average
precision, $\mathrm{nAP} = (\mathrm{AP} - \pi)/(1 - \pi)$, where $\pi$ is the test prevalence:
0 is the score of a random ranking and 1 a perfect one. Within-lineage discrimination is the
mean, over STs containing both phenotypes, of the AUROC inside each ST; a score constant within
each ST gets exactly 0.5. Confidence intervals are 95\% paired bootstrap intervals (2,000
replicates) resampling whole clusters: STs internally, BioProjects externally, SNP clusters in
\textit{Campylobacter}.

\subsection*{External cohort}
The NCBI Pathogen Detection \Ecoli{}/\textit{Shigella} isolate table (PDG000000004.6395) was
filtered to isolates with submitted susceptibility results, called with AMRFinderPlus 4.2.7, and
not matching any of our strains by run, BioSample or assembly accession. The primary cohort uses
the same database version as training (10,232 isolates in 62 BioProjects); all 4.2.7 isolates
(10,876) are a sensitivity analysis. Models were trained once on all called training strains and
applied unchanged; external genes absent from training were dropped. CTX was tested on only 1,054
external isolates, 70\% resistant, and is reported separately from the primary mean.

\subsection*{MIC harmonisation}
MICs, measurement signs and testing standards for the external cohort were exported from the
Pathogen Detection AST browser (171,750 rows; all 10,232 isolates matched by PDT accession).
Censored values were turned into intervals ($\le v \mapsto (0, v]$; $< v \mapsto (0, v/2]$;
$\ge v \mapsto [v, \infty)$; $> v \mapsto [2v, \infty)$), and an isolate was labelled with the
EUCAST v16.1 Enterobacterales breakpoints \cite{eucast2026v161} only when the whole interval fell on
one side of a breakpoint. Trimethoprim--sulfamethoxazole values reported as the sulfamethoxazole
component (19 times a trimethoprim dilution) were divided by 19. CTX had fewer than 1,000 MICs and
was not analysed.

\subsection*{Second species}
Every organism folder in Pathogen Detection (107) was screened with a pre-registered gate: at least
2,000 isolates with susceptibility results and AMRFinderPlus 4.2.7 calls, at least three drugs with
100 resistant and 100 susceptible isolates, SNP-cluster membership for at least 80\% of isolates
with at least 50 clusters, and assembly statistics for at least 80\%. One organism passed,
\textit{Campylobacter} (4,426 isolates). Its determinant rule (gyrA point mutations for
ciprofloxacin and nalidixic acid; tet genes for tetracycline; 23S rRNA point mutations or erm genes
for clindamycin) was taken from the literature before any score was computed
\cite{zhao2016campylobacter,iovine2013campylobacter}. Folds were grouped by SNP cluster.

\subsection*{Clinical error rates}
Following the FDA guidance for susceptibility-testing systems \cite{fda2009ast}, a major error is a
susceptible isolate called resistant (as a share of susceptible isolates), a very major error a
resistant isolate called susceptible (as a share of resistant isolates), and categorical agreement
the share of correct calls. Learned models were called resistant at a score of 0.5, fixed in
advance. The guidance's benchmarks (categorical agreement $> 89.9\%$, major error $< 3\%$, upper
95\% confidence limit of very major error $< 7.5\%$) are reported as context only: they were
written for phenotypic devices, not genomic prediction.

\subsection*{Pre-registration}
Each experiment (W6--W14) has a document stating its question, design, endpoint and pass rule,
committed before its data were read; later amendments are dated and were also committed before the
data they concern. Table~\ref{tab:prereg} lists every pre-registered endpoint and its outcome.

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Random splits are dominated by signal that is not resistance biology}

On the random split the full stack reached a mean average precision of 0.850 and the classical arm
0.846. A model that never saw the genome sequence, using only metadata and assembly statistics,
reached 0.705 (Figure~\ref{fig:floor}). The metadata signal did not survive any split that moved
populations: across countries and across time, country, year, study and platform together scored
an \nap{} between 0.000 and 0.056. Assembly statistics alone retained an \nap{} of 0.097--0.232 on
those splits. On the lineage holdout the full stack reached 0.645 and metadata 0.297. The random
split, in short, measures a mixture in which a large part of the apparent accuracy is available
without reading any gene.

\subsection*{Determinant calls rescue prediction on unseen lineages}

With ST-grouped five-fold cross-validation, the pan-genome catalogue alone reached a mean \nap{}
of 0.450 over the three drugs whose determinants are acquired (CTX, CAZ, GEN). Adding each
strain's determinant calls raised it to 0.726, a pre-registered difference of $+0.276$
[0.178, 0.328], while CIP, whose determinants are point mutations already represented in the
catalogue, did not change ($-0.004$; Figure~\ref{fig:rescue}). The rescue held with intermediates
excluded ($+0.293$), with all database versions ($+0.286$), on the country splits
(UK$\rightarrow$Norway 0.249$\rightarrow$0.429; Norway$\rightarrow$UK 0.543$\rightarrow$0.793),
on the temporal split (0.449$\rightarrow$0.670), and inside each country separately (United
Kingdom $+0.256$ [0.119, 0.457]; Norway $+0.308$ [0.190, 0.363]). The determinant calls alone
scored as well as calls plus catalogue (0.730): on unseen lineages the catalogue added nothing for
these drugs.

The rescue is demonstrated on the 5,067 strains that have both a catalogue profile and published
calls. A pre-registered check of whether the catalogue-only collapse is equally present in the
1,890 strains without calls failed: there the catalogue scored 0.641 against 0.449 in the called
strains. Most of the uncalled strains (1,439) come from one study, and in that stratum metadata
alone predicted the cephalosporin phenotypes well (CTX \nap{} 0.735), which suggests a
study-specific structure; the analysis was not designed to separate the two explanations and we
do not claim either.

\subsection*{Assembly size is a within-lineage proxy for accessory content}

Inside sequence types, assembly length correlated with the number of acquired resistance genes
(within-ST Spearman $\rho = 0.325$; 4,033 strains, 62 STs) and ranked resistant above susceptible
strains of the same ST (within-ST concordance 0.552--0.676 across the seven drugs). To test whether
this is a batch artefact, we conditioned on the submitting study as well: within
(ST$\times$study) cells the concordance of assembly length over the acquired-determinant drugs
fell only from 0.613 to 0.592, and its correlation with acquired-gene count from 0.325 to 0.321
(Figure~\ref{fig:assembly}). The signal is carried by content statistics, not technical ones:
contig N50 was below 0.5 for every drug (0.383--0.490) and correlated negatively with acquired-gene
count ($\rho = -0.185$), as expected if larger, more fragmented assemblies carry more plasmid DNA.
In \textit{Campylobacter} the same signal appeared for tetracycline, whose tet(O) gene is commonly
plasmid-borne \cite{iovine2013campylobacter} (within-cluster concordance 0.832), and not for the
chromosomal point-mutation drugs (0.450--0.549). In the external cohort, a model on assembly
statistics alone reached a mean \nap{} of 0.045.

\subsection*{Models trained on two countries transfer to an external cohort}

The external cohort differs from the training collection in every way we could measure: 68\% of
isolates are from the United States, 57\% from food or animal sources, and the median collection
year is 2019 (Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}). Gradient boosting on all determinant calls, trained on our
strains and applied unchanged, reached a mean \nap{} of 0.807 over six drugs, against 0.045 for
the assembly arm: a pre-registered difference of $+0.762$ [0.676, 0.804], 62 BioProjects
(Figure~\ref{fig:external}). The result held on all database versions ($+0.773$) and with
intermediates excluded ($+0.753$). Dropping each of the nine BioProjects with at least 100
labelled isolates in turn left the difference between 0.723 and 0.777, with no interval falling
below 0.643. No external isolate matched any identifier of our strains.

\subsection*{A sparse linear model on determinant calls is enough where a model helps}

On unseen lineages, a learned model on determinant calls beat the determinant rule:
$+0.122$ [0.075, 0.197] mean \nap{} over CTX, CAZ and GEN, with every per-drug interval above zero.
The L1 logistic regression did as well as gradient boosting (difference $-0.011$
[$-0.026$, 0.007], inside the pre-registered equivalence margin of $\pm0.05$) and beat the rule by
$+0.137$ [0.097, 0.203] (Figure~\ref{fig:rule}).

Externally, the picture depends on the model. Gradient boosting beat the rule by only $+0.065$
[0.005, 0.140], and lost to it for GEN and CTX. The linear model, trained on the same features,
beat the rule by $+0.104$ [0.060, 0.166], was at or above it for every drug, and was ahead of
gradient boosting on six of seven drugs (mean $+0.038$ [0.019, 0.063]; by our pre-registered margin
of 0.05 this comparison is inconclusive). The two drugs on which the rule beat gradient boosting
are therefore failures of that model, not of learning from determinant calls.

Label heterogeneity also hides part of the margin. Re-labelling the external isolates with EUCAST
breakpoints from their MICs raised the margin of gradient boosting over the rule from $+0.065$ to
$+0.123$ [0.081, 0.174]. Because EUCAST labels exist for more isolates than submitter calls, we
repeated the comparison on the 35,367 isolate--drug pairs that have both: the margin rose from
$+0.064$ [$-0.009$, 0.159] to $+0.101$ [0.066, 0.158] (exploratory, not pre-registered).

The advantage of a model is not general. In \textit{Campylobacter}, where resistance to the four
eligible drugs is explained by gyrA T86I, tet(O) and 23S rRNA mutations
\cite{zhao2016campylobacter}, the rule reached a mean \nap{} of 0.928 and gradient boosting 0.922;
the pre-registered difference was $-0.006$ [$-0.017$, 0.004].

\subsection*{Very major errors remain the unsolved problem}

At a fixed threshold, with EUCAST labels, categorical agreement over the six external drugs was
0.914 for gradient boosting, 0.938 for the linear model and 0.942 for the rule, and major error
rates were 2.7\%, 1.7\% and 2.7\%. Very major error rates were 25.1\%, 18.7\% and 14.4\%; no
approach met the FDA benchmark, and only the rule for ciprofloxacin did so for a single drug
(Table~\ref{tab:clinical}, Figure~\ref{fig:clinical}). Amoxicillin--clavulanic acid was the worst
drug for every approach (very major error 35--50\%), as the uncatalogued variation in its
resistance genes predicts \cite{lipworth2024uncatalogued}. The learned models ranked resistant
isolates better than the rule but, at the pre-stated threshold, missed more of them; we did not
move the threshold after seeing this. Errors were more frequent near the breakpoint than far from
it, but by less than the pre-registered factor of two (ratio 1.64 for gradient boosting, 1.87 for
the rule); for ciprofloxacin the model's errors were almost all within one dilution of the
breakpoint, whereas for gentamicin the model made errors far from it (18.1\% against 2.7\% for the
rule).

\subsection*{The neural architecture adds a small, seed-stable increment}

On the lineage holdout the full stack exceeded the classical arm by $+0.028$ [0.006, 0.052] mean
\nap{}. Retraining the neural branch with two further seeds gave $+0.020$ [0.003, 0.042] and
$+0.018$ [$-0.001$, 0.037] (mean $+0.022$, SD 0.005), which passes the pre-registered stability rule
(positive mean, interval above zero in two of three seeds). The neural branch alone stayed below
the classical arm in every seed (0.405--0.435 against 0.556). The architecture is therefore one
small, reproducible contribution to the stack, not the source of the findings above.

\section*{Discussion}

The results separate three regimes. Where the determinants of a drug are few and nearly sufficient,
as for gentamicin and cefotaxime in surveillance isolates or for all four eligible drugs in
\textit{Campylobacter}, a fixed rule is as good as any model we tried. Where they are numerous,
interacting or incompletely catalogued, as for amoxicillin--clavulanic acid and for the
cephalosporins on unseen \Ecoli{} lineages, a model on the determinant calls adds substantially,
and a sparse linear model is enough. And where the determinants are missing from the features,
as for a reference pan-genome catalogue on unseen lineages, no amount of modelling recovers them;
models then lean on proxies such as assembly size, which carry real biology within a collection
but do not transfer.

This has three practical consequences for reporting genomic resistance prediction. A determinant
rule built from a public catalogue should be the baseline every model is compared with, per drug.
A model on assembly statistics and metadata alone should be reported on every split, because it
measures how much of an accuracy figure is available without reading a gene. And claims of
generalisation need lineage-disjoint and, ideally, external evaluation; random splits overstated
the contribution of non-genomic signal by a large margin here. Our observation that gradient
boosting transferred worse than a sparse linear model on identical features also argues for
reporting a simple learned baseline next to any complex one. These recommendations are consistent
with earlier work on population structure \cite{yu2025biased}, rule-versus-model benchmarks
\cite{hu2024assessing} and feature representation under lineage shift
\cite{fistarol2025copynumber,moradigaravand2018prediction}; what this study adds is a pre-registered
decomposition on one collection, an external test with harmonised labels, and a second species
that shows where the advantage of a model ends.

\subsection*{Limitations}
The training collection is two-country and research-derived. Determinant calls were available for
73\% of training strains, and the uncalled strains behave differently (W10, part A failed), so the
rescue is shown on the called subset. The external cohort is dominated by surveillance isolates
from the United States, and its labels come from many laboratories; harmonising to EUCAST helps
but is not a reference-method comparison. Only one second species met our gate, and it is one in
which resistance is close to monogenic. Clinical error rates were computed at a single, pre-stated
threshold on uncalibrated scores. The neural architecture was not tuned for the analyses here, and
none of the learned models was tuned at all; better models may exist, but the comparisons are fair
because every model saw the same features and folds.
